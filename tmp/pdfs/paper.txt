Abstract
Protecting the intellectual property of open-weight large language models (LLMs)
1
requires verifying whether a suspect model is derived from a victim model de-
2
spite common laundering operations such as fine-tuning (including PPO/DPO),
3
pruning/compression, and model merging.
We propose ATTNDIFF, a data-
4
efficient white-box framework that extracts fingerprints from models via intrin-
5
sic information-routing behavior. ATTNDIFF probes minimally edited prompt
6
pairs that induce controlled semantic conflicts, captures differential attention
7
patterns, summarizes them with compact spectral descriptors, and compares
8
models using CKA. Across Llama-2/3 and Qwen2.5 (3B–14B) and additional
9
open-source families, it yields high similarity for related derivatives while sep-
10
arating unrelated model families (e.g., > 0.98 vs. < 0.22 with M
= 60
11
probes). With 5–60 multi-domain probes, it supports practical provenance ver-
12
ification and accountability; our open-source implementation is available at
13
https://anonymous.4open.science/r/AttnDiff-37A1/.

Introduction
15
The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs), such as the Llama series [46], Qwen [3],
16
and DeepSeek [28], has revolutionized natural language understanding and generation. However,
17
the prohibitive costs associated with large-scale data curation and massive computational resources
18
render model weights the core intellectual property (IP) of AI entities. In the current open-weight
19
ecosystem, the risk of unauthorized redistribution, illicit fine-tuning, and model laundering has
20
escalated significantly [57, 67]. Consequently, model provenance verification—the forensic capability
21
to trace a suspect model back to its architectural or parametric origin—has become a cornerstone for
22
copyright enforcement and IP protection [57, 17].
23
Existing methodologies for model ownership verification are generally bifurcated into invasive and
24
non-invasive (passive) approaches [57]. Invasive techniques, such as backdoor watermark-based
25
fingerprints, embed ownership signals by modifying the training process [56, 51, 25, 35]; however,
26
they necessarily alter model weights, inducing a trade-off between fingerprint capacity and utility and
27
potentially introducing hard-to-characterize vulnerabilities, such as spurious trigger activations or
28
degraded generalization [52, 56, 51]. Moreover, if the model is stolen before embedding, invasive
29
mechanisms do not support retrospective provenance analysis, which is particularly problematic in
30
open-weight, post-hoc forensic settings where released models typically lack pre-embedded protection
31
and thus cannot be reliably traced ex post.
32
In contrast, non-invasive approaches—commonly referred to as intrinsic model fingerprinting—
33
extract signatures without altering parameters. Existing intrinsic fingerprints fall into four families
34
with distinct robustness gaps: parameter-based fingerprints [66] operate in weight space and are
35
fragile under structural edits such as structured pruning (Sec. 5.4); representation-based finger-

42
Motivated by these limitations, we propose ATTNDIFF, a robust and data-efficient white-box finger-
43
printing framework based on differential attention dynamics under semantic conflict, i.e., minimally
44
perturbed prompt pairs that invert logical entailment. Rather than relying on static parameters, generic
45
hidden states, or surface-level semantics, ATTNDIFF directly probes how a model re-routes attention
46
when confronted with such prompts. Our central hypothesis is that the resulting information-routing
47
pattern constitutes a stable, intrinsic property of the model. Specifically, this pattern—defined by
48
how self-attention distributes attention weights over tokens encoding logical operators, factual priors,
49
and safety signals—persists under a broad spectrum of model-level attacks. Concretely, ATTNDIFF
50
constructs paired prompts that introduce small lexical pivots, such as single-word substitutions, to
51
flip the underlying entailment (e.g., “parallel lines never intersect” vs. “always intersect”). It then
52
extracts the corresponding differential attention maps across layers and heads, compressing them
53
into compact spectral descriptors. Models are compared in this feature space using centered linear
54
CKA [22], a similarity measure that captures structural alignment between representation spaces and
55
is invariant to isotropic reparameterization. The resulting fingerprints are simultaneously robust to
56
realistic model-level attacks and discriminative across architecturally distinct models.

Related work
58
2.1
Invasive fingerprints
59
Invasive fingerprints embed ownership signals by modifying models during (pre-)training, typically
60
via weight watermarks or backdoor triggers. Weight watermark-based schemes encode verifiable
61
patterns in parameter space [69, 15, 10, 29, 43] but face capacity–utility trade-offs and may lose
62
robustness under pruning, quantization, or strong fine-tuning [57]. Backdoor-based fingerprints use
63
trigger datasets for black-box attribution [51, 5, 59, 56, 53, 58, 26, 42], yet require training-time
64
control and can be removed by targeted erasure [68]. Recent work explores scalable fingerprint
65
transfer [55, 54], harmless editing [65], and adversarial robustness [49].
66
2.2
Intrinsic fingerprints
67
Non-invasive (intrinsic) fingerprints do not inject external signals and instead mine model-inherent
68
properties for provenance verification [57]. Representative families include parameter-space statistics
69
in weight space [66, 17], representation-based descriptors over hidden states/logits [67, 63, 45],
70
semantic output traces [37, 50], and adversarial-example-based triggers [21, 14]. However, these
71
signals can be brittle under realistic transformations, e.g., pruning/architecture edits, distillation, or
72
preference optimization, and/or sensitive to decoding and input perturbations, limiting robustness
73
across fine-tuning, pruning/merging, and distillation.
74
Among intrinsic approaches, ATTNDIFF departs from parameter-, representation-, and output-level
75
signatures by fingerprinting models through their differential attention responses to controlled
76
semantic conflicts, with similarity measured via CKA.

4
Design of ATTNDIFF
94
4.1
Motivation
95
In post-hoc LLM forensics, a suspect is rarely an “exact-copy”: stolen checkpoints are routinely
96
laundered through fine-tuning/alignment, pruning, merging, or distillation. A practical fingerprint
97
should therefore remain stable under such transformations, where weight-space statistics can break
98
under structural edits and hidden-state/logit signatures can drift after strong adaptation.
99
We encountered an empirical turning point while stress-testing small, highly controlled probes. We
100
constructed minimally edited origin/corrupted prompt pairs that preserve surface form while flipping
101
semantics via a single-word lexical pivot. Under these “controlled semantic conflicts”, attention did
102
not drift arbitrarily; models re-allocated attention in a structured manner. More importantly, this
103
re-allocation appeared family-consistent: Llama-2-7B [46] derivatives exhibited closely matched
104
profiles, whereas an unrelated model family such as Qwen2.5-7B [38] followed a systematically
105
different regime. Together, these observations suggested that a model’s intrinsic information-routing
106
strategy—how it redistributes self-attention when resolving a semantic contradiction—may encode
107
lineage-specific structure that survives common “laundering” operations. A detailed pilot study
108
quantifying these re-allocation patterns via interpretable structural statistics (concentration, entropy,
109
locality) is presented in Appendix E.
110
These observations motivate ATTNDIFF: by probing a model with minimally edited prompt pairs that
111
induce controlled semantic conflicts, we can elicit a characteristic re-routing response that is stable
112
within a model lineage yet distinct across unrelated families. The next subsection describes how we
113
operationalize this idea into a practical fingerprinting pipeline.
114
4.2
ATTNDIFF workflow
115
Notation. We denote a model with L layers and H attention heads per layer. For a prompt p tokenized
116

l,h ∈RN×N represents the post-softmax causal self-attention probability matrix
117

121
ATTNDIFF fingerprints LLMs by capturing their differential attention dynamics under semantic
122
conflict. As shown in Figure 1, the pipeline comprises three stages: (i) Probe construction: generate
123
M origin/corrupted prompt pairs via single-word pivot substitutions; (ii) Fingerprint extraction:
124
extract differential attention maps ∆A across layers and heads, compress them into spectral descrip-
125
tors via SVD, and stack into fingerprint matrix F ∈RM×D; (iii) Similarity measurement: compare
126
two models by computing centered kernel alignment (CKA) on their fingerprint matrices. Given a
127
victim model and a suspect model, we compute a similarity score s = CKA(F, F ′) to quantify their
128
functional similarity. Implementation details are provided in Appendix B.

5.3
Model merge
231
Model merging combines multiple pretrained models in weight space or at the distribution/behavior
232
level, often integrating complementary capabilities without accessing training data or retraining [61,
233
1, 47, 48, 64, 18]. Because a merged suspect is derived from multiple victim models, its fingerprints
234
can be mixed and harder to attribute to all sources.
235
From Table 2, ATTNDIFF consistently attains high similarity with all parent models in weight-space
236
merges (≥0.95) and maintains substantial similarity (≈0.78–0.80) with heterogeneous distribution-
237
level merges, whereas several baselines either fail to attribute all sources or exhibit noticeably weaker
238
alignment on cross-architecture merges.

Contributions. We propose a white-box, post-hoc fingerprinting method that captures model-specific
304
information-routing behavior via differential attention under controlled semantic conflicts. We
305
represent each model with compact spectral descriptors and compare fingerprints using centered
306
linear CKA, enabling architecture-agnostic similarity that remains stable for co-originated derivatives
307
and well separated from unrelated models under common laundering operations.
308
Probe refresh. Beyond average-case robustness, ATTNDIFF is compatible with operational probe
309
refresh: a defender can update or expand the probe set to reduce attack transfer without changing
310
the reference model. This is important in post-hoc auditing settings where a suspect may have been
311
adapted after release and where the defender can recompute fingerprints on demand.
312
Computational cost. ATTNDIFF requires no training or gradient-based optimization. With M
313
probe pairs, fingerprint extraction requires 2M forward passes per model (origin + corrupted) while
314
recording attention; post-processing (pooling when needed, differencing, top-K SVD, and CKA) is
315
lightweight. The victim fingerprint can be cached, so per-suspect cost is dominated by the suspect’s
316
2M forward passes. On a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU, ATTNDIFF fingerprinting with M = 60
317
probe pairs takes approximately 5 minutes for a 7B-parameter model. Compared with baselines,
318
ATTNDIFF incurs modest inference cost without heavyweight operations such as tensor construction
319
and alignment heuristics (PCS/ICS), trigger optimization (ProFlingo), or inference model training
320
(LLMMap). A detailed computational comparison is provided in Appendix D.3.
321
7
Conclusion
322
We present ATTNDIFF, a white-box post-hoc fingerprinting method that captures model-specific
323
information-routing behavior via differential attention under controlled semantic conflicts. Using
324
compact spectral descriptors and centered linear CKA, it yields model-agnostic fingerprints that
325
remain stable for co-originated derivatives and well separated from unrelated architectures across
326
fine-tuning (including PPO/DPO), pruning/compression, and model merging. With a small probe
327
set and lightweight computation, ATTNDIFF provides a practical building block for provenance
328
verification and accountability in the open-weight LLM ecosystem. Nevertheless, the adoption of
329
such provenance tools also entails societal risks, such as the potential for unwarranted copyright
330
disputes (see Appendix G for a detailed discussion).
331
Limitations and future work
332
White-box access. ATTNDIFF currently assumes a white-box setting: extracting fingerprints requires
333
access to internal attention activations or equivalent hidden-state signals. Consequently, the method
334
does not directly apply to strictly black-box APIs where only model outputs are observable.
335
Theoretical modeling of laundering effects. While the centered linear CKA comparison provides
336
useful invariance to feature rotations, permutations, and isotropic scaling, we still lack a complete
337
mathematical model that propagates common laundering operations (e.g., fine-tuning, pruning,
338
merging, and distillation) from parameter or architecture transformations to perturbations in the
339
extracted ATTNDIFF fingerprints and the resulting similarity scores. Establishing such an end-to-end
340
modeling framework remains an important direction for future work.

A
Theoretical guarantees of ATTNDIFF
566
We provide a theoretical analysis of ATTNDIFF centered on the centered linear CKA similarity. Our
567
goal is to justify: (i) why centered linear CKA is suitable for comparing ATTNDIFF fingerprints even
568
when the feature dimensions differ (D ̸= D′); and (ii) why common post-training transformations
569
often preserve high similarity for related models.
570
Roadmap.
571
• CKA preliminaries (Appendix. A.1). We summarize the definition of centered linear CKA
572
and its key invariance properties.
573
• Compatibility of ATTNDIFF with CKA (Appendix. A.2). We discuss why the ATTNDIFF
574
extraction procedure aligns with these invariances and is typically stable under common
575
post-training transformations.
576
• Perturbation stability (Appendix. A.3). We give a coarse bound showing that CKA
577
remains close to 1 when the centered probe-wise Gram matrices are close.
578
Together, these results clarify why CKA is suitable for comparing ATTNDIFF fingerprints across
579
heterogeneous feature dimensions.

A.2
Robustness of AttnDiff fingerprint extraction
608
AttnDiff robustness stems from three design principles: (i) differential attention ∆A = ˜A −A
609
suppresses prompt-specific baselines, (ii) SVD-based TopK(σ) descriptors capture dominant re-
610
routing structure, and (iii) centered linear CKA measures similarity in probe-indexed Gram space.
611
These components collectively yield invariance properties that enable stable provenance verification.
612
Differential cancellation.
Attention maps decompose into prompt-dependent baselines (token
613
positions, syntax, domain priors) plus conflict-induced re-routing. Computing ∆A = ˜A −A cancels
614
shared baselines and isolates the re-routing signal. Under adaptation (e.g., domain-specific fine-
615
tuning), baselines shift but the differential re-routing pattern—governed by intrinsic information-flow
616
architecture—remains stable, reducing sensitivity to domain drift.
617
Spectral invariances.
Singular values of ∆A are invariant to orthogonal token-space transfor-
618
mations and capture dominant energy distribution. Under transformations preserving the routing
619
subspace (near-orthogonal re-parameterizations from weight updates, global scaling from normal-
620
ization), fingerprints F undergo rotations/permutations/isotropic scaling. Proposition 1 shows such
621
changes leave centered linear CKA invariant.

C
Probe-aware Suppression Attack
806
We discuss an adaptive evasion scenario where the attacker has obtained a partial subset of the
807
defender’s probe pairs and is aware of the AttnDiff extraction procedure. The attacker then performs
808
probe-aware training to suppress the differential attention signal on the leaked probes, e.g., by
809
explicitly penalizing the differential attention maps ∆A and/or their spectral descriptors (such as the
810
ℓ2 norm of TopK(σ)), so that the resulting fingerprints become less informative under these probes.
811
Mitigation. A practical mitigation is to maintain a larger private probe pool and refresh probes
812
over time. In verification, the defender can evaluate on held-out probes that were not exposed to
813
the attacker and periodically introduce new contradiction templates and domains. Under such probe
814
refresh, suppression tuned to a fixed leaked subset is unlikely to generalize to the full and evolving
815
probe distribution, and the held-out probes provide a direct check against probe-specific overfitting.
